
** The Radical History Of Lisp

The first Lisp was a bold experiment

- 1600s - 1970s :: *A Computer is a Human Being* who does (primarily
  numerical) mathematical work.  *Various devices are invented to
  assist these computers*, e.g. abacuses, sliderules, early mechanical
  calculators and eventually electromechanical calculators.
- 1820s :: The [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath][Polymath]] [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Babbage][Charles Babbage]] invents [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_engine][The Difference Engine]],
  an enormously powerful mechanical calculator for its time.
- 1837 :: [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Babbage][Charles Babbage]] describes and tries to create [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_engine][The Analytical
  Engine]] - the first (awkwardly) programmable digital computer.
  Despite being heavily funded by the British Government, this effort
  fails - its Base-10 mechanisms are too complex for the mechanical
  arts of the time.
- Late 1830s :: The mathemamatician [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_Lovelace][Ada Lovelace]] (oh what a colorful
  history she had!) realized that Babbage's Analytical Engine could do
  more than just calculate numbers: It could calculate with symbols,
  and one of the things it could represent with symbols could be its
  own programs!  She wrote the world's first computer program - before
  any computers existed!
- mid-1930s :: [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konrad_Zuse][Konrad Zuse]] invents the world's first programmable
  computer in Berlin during World War II.  He was insprired by
  Babbage's Analytical Engine but realized it could be greatly
  simplified by using the Binary Number System instead of Decimal.
  Binary was invented by Leibniz who the English were still hating
  because the great Isaac Newton accused Leibniz of plagiarizing
  Newton's Calculus.  Using Leibniz's Binary system, Zuse succeeded
  where Babbage had failed.  Zuze went on to invent an early (possibly
  the first) high-level programming language and computer operating
  system.  Alas, Zuse's work was ignored in the UK and America and did
  not influence the development of modern computers.
- 1936 :: The mathematician [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alonzo_Church][Alonzo Church]] invents the [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambda_calculus][The Lambda
  Calculus]] to describe the general idea of *Procedures: Sequences of
  operations which transform inputs into desired outputs.* A procedure
  is like a recipe, but working with abstract mathematical objects.
  When a procedure can be mathematically proven to produce a desired
  result, it is called [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithm][an Algorthm - aka an Effective Procedure]].  A
  special part of the Alonzo Church's Lambda Calculus was *Lambda: The
  Procedure which Creates procedures!* The Lambda Calculus was
  invented to be used by Mathematicans to prove theorems about what
  computability in the abstract, e.g. by human computers.  It was not
  designed to be understood by machines!
- late-1930s :: Under World War II military secrecy, the English
  mathematician (and more) [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing][Alan Turing]] helps invent the first
  stored-program general-purpose computer.  That work remained secret
  and instead of the British Government recognizing him as a hero and
  a genius, Turing was persecuted for being homosexual.  Even though
  he'd gotten his doctorate under Alonzo Church, Turing didn't use the
  Lambda Calculus, instead inventing a system much more like a
  physical machine, now called a [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_machine][Turing Machine.]]  Despite his
  persecution and lack of full recognition interrupting his direct
  involvement in the development of computing in Britain (oh boy did
  that cost them!), Turing is now considered the "Father of Computer
  Science."  His mathematical work on the theory of computing was
  hugely influential and his Turing Machine influenced the design of
  all modern computers.
- Mid-1940s :: Programmable electronic general-purpose electronic
  (vacuum tube!) computers using the binary number systems are
  develped in Britain and the United States, gradually developing the
  architecture of the computers we use today.
- Mid-1940s to early 1950s :: Programmers vs. Coders.  The programs of
  early electronic computers were written in the low-level binary
  language they directly understood.  Writing such programs was
  extremely tedious, so high-status computing profesionals called
  "Programmers" described computer programs in loose [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudocode][Pseudocode
  Notations]], mixtures of English, mathematical notation, diagrams or
  whatever they felt made clear what they wanted the computer to do.
  These "Programmers" would then give these pseudocode descriptions of
  what they wanted to "Coders" who had lower status and pay to turn
  into binary programs.  Some of these "coders" invented [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assembly_language][low-level
  Assembly Languages]] and programs called [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assembly_language#Assembler][Assemblers]] which would
  automatically translate programs written in Assembly Language into
  programs written in the computer's built-in binary language.  Thus
  Ada Lovelace's prophetic dream was first achieved by "Coders"
  (including many women) rather than "Programmers" (always men)!.
  Programming in Assembly Language was easier, but still tedious.
  Also, every model of computer had a different binary machine
  language paired with its own Assembly Languuage, so programs written
  for one model of computer could not be transferred to a different
  model.
- Mid 1950s ::  [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McCarthy_(computer_scientist)][John McCarthy]], a prestigious computer scientist,
  invented [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M-expression][M-Expressions]] based on the Lambda Calculus as a [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudocode][Pseudocode
  Notation]] for describing the procecdures composing Computer Programs.
